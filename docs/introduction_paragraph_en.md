Complex human diseases rarely arise from the dysfunction of a single gene; rather, they typically
emerge from coordinated disruptions involving multiple disease-associated genes and their molecular
products \cite{Tian2021EMODMI}. From a network medicine perspective, these disease-relevant genes
and proteins are not randomly distributed across the molecular interaction network; instead, they
tend to cluster together and cooperate in related biological functions
\cite{DelSol2010NetworkPerturbations}. Such observations support the concept of *disease
modules*—coherent subnetworks of the human interactome enriched for genes or proteins implicated in
the same disease—whose connectivity patterns in protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks provide a
natural substrate for systematic disease module detection \cite{Ghiassian2015DIAMOnD}. Understanding
and reliably identifying these modules is therefore an important step towards mechanistic
interpretation of disease biology and the prioritization of candidate therapeutic targets.
